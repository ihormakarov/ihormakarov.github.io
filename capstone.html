CHAPTER 1≈2 MIN

Arms for Oil

Eighty years of one trade: American protection for Gulf energy. Then America stopped needing the energy.

The original bargain1945–2010s

Washington gave

  • Security guarantees — a doctrine, a command, a coalition
  • Arms — U.S. platforms still dominate Gulf arsenals
⇄

The Gulf gave

  • Reliable oil — insurance against shortage and shock
  • Access — basing and logistics for the wider region

The bargain begins in secret. In February 1945, fresh from Yalta, Franklin D. Roosevelt met King — hidden from the British. Fearing oil shortages, America secured Saudi reserves; Riyadh got protection from great and regional powers. One exchange, half a century of entanglement.

After the 1973 embargo, the 1979 Iranian revolution, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, the declared the Gulf a vital interest to be defended by force; made the threat credible. When Iraq seized Kuwait in 1990, a U.S.-led coalition of 42 countries — the largest since World War II — reversed it.

By the 2010s the trade had gone lopsided. The made America a hydrocarbon superpower in its own right, yet the Gulf still shops overwhelmingly American for defense — and Washington had ever less reason to keep paying the security bill.

That is the puzzle this story answers: the Gulf needed to re-manufacture America’s reason to stay.

Compute Is the New Oil

Frontier AI runs on electricity America is struggling to deliver. The Gulf noticed.

Why compute migratesThe mismatch

America’s bottlenecks

  • ~5-year waits to plug new power into the grid
  • 2.8-year average environmental reviews
  • Rising bills — and rising voter anger
⇄

The Gulf’s offer

  • 3.2–7.4¢/kWh industrial power
  • State-cleared land, grid, and permits

Why gigawatt campuses? : AI performance improves predictably with more data, parameters, and . Training compute has doubled roughly every six months since 2010 — for frontier models, up to 2.5× per six months since 2020. Epoch AI credits about two-thirds of AI gains to compute scaling, with the power to train and deploy models doubling yearly. The American bottleneck

The constraint isn’t chips — it’s the grid. queues average about five years, and nearly 80% of the capacity queued between 2000 and 2019 was never connected at all. impact statements average 2.8 years; more than 650 projects sat awaiting permits in 2025 alone. Both administrations have named these delays threats to American AI ambitions.

And the politics are turning

Where data centers cluster, bills follow. In — the grid region that includes Northern Virginia — capacity prices rose nine-fold in three delivery years — $29 to $329 per megawatt-day. Northern Virginia electricity climbed almost 270% in five years; U.S. prices are forecast up another 40% by 2030. The backlash is bipartisan: a Sanders–Ocasio-Cortez federal moratorium bill, a New York bill, Republican restriction bills in Georgia, Maryland, Oklahoma, South Carolina, and South Dakota. None looks likely to pass — but the constituency for unlimited buildout is thinning.

The Deal

May 2025: two announcements that put frontier American silicon on Gulf soil for the first time.

The architectureMay 2025

Washington clears

  • Blackwell exports — first time beyond close allies
  • U.S. giants in: OpenAI, Microsoft, AWS, Nvidia, xAI
⇄

The Gulf builds

  • 5 GW Stargate UAE campus, built by G42
  • 6.6 GW Saudi capacity target by 2034, with Nvidia’s $10B in Humain

Presidents Trump and Mohamed bin Zayed unveiled a campus of 5 gigawatts maximum capacity — built by the UAE’s , 1 GW in Phase 1 — the most powerful AI campus announced outside the United States. In Riyadh, a strategic AI partnership: Nvidia investing $10 billion in , Saudi Arabia’s state AI company, targeting 6.6 GW by 2034.

The historic part isn’t the concrete — it’s the silicon: Nvidia’s frontier generation, cleared for the first time for countries outside America’s circle of close allies. In November 2025, after six months of deliberation, Commerce authorized 35,000 GB300-class chips each for Humain and G42 — conditioned on strict security and reporting requirements whose details remain unpublished.

How big? The honest scale

Then the catch: announced gigawatts are not authorized chips. Early reporting had Washington weighing half a million Blackwells a year for the UAE — roughly 500–700 MW annually. What Commerce actually approved in November: ~49 MW per country. At that pace, 5 GW takes at least 50 years.

Yet the momentum is real — and one-sided

While chips crawled through Commerce, everything else moved. By December 2025, Mubadala’s Khaldoon Al Mubarak confirmed 5,000 workers on site in Abu Dhabi. Humain stacked deals with Microsoft, AWS, Nvidia, and xAI — Amazon alone pledged $5.3 billion for an AWS region, xAI a 500+ MW facility, Nvidia up to 500 MW by 2030. Elsewhere, projects stalled: by April 2026, OpenAI had paused UK over energy costs and regulation, dropped the 230-MW Norway site — and scrapped a 600-MW expansion at flagship Abilene, Texas. Project by project, the Gulf is becoming the only option still moving.

CHAPTER 4≈2.5 MIN

Manufacturing Dependency

What the Gulf is really buying: a reason for America to stay — the quest for a silicon shield.

The Gulf’s tradeThe silicon shield

The Gulf puts in

  • $2T+ in investment pledges into the U.S.
  • Equity across the U.S. AI stack — labs to land
⇄

The Gulf wants back

  • De facto protection — troops, air defense, interceptors
  • A post-oil economy built on exporting

The central claim: Riyadh and Abu Dhabi aren’t merely hosting American compute — they’re cultivating American dependence: AI machinery doing for the 2020s what oil did for 1945. Once hyperscaler billions and frontier silicon sit on Gulf sand, defending them pulls in troops, air defenses, drone interceptors — protection reaching well beyond the data centers. No treaty required: Al Udeid Air Base never came with formal guarantees either, and deterred all the same.

The quieter lever: equity

Pledges make headlines; ownership makes leverage. By early 2026, Abu Dhabi’s held stakes in all three U.S. frontier labs — OpenAI’s $6.6B 2024 round, xAI’s $20B Series E in January 2026, Anthropic’s $30B February 2026 round at a $380B valuation. Humain put ~$3B into xAI; Aramco plans a stake in Humain itself — oil revenue flowing into the U.S. AI stack. In October 2025, MGX joined BlackRock, Microsoft, Nvidia, and xAI buying Aligned Data Centers for $40B — the largest digital-infrastructure deal on record. The same actors hosting U.S. compute abroad now own pieces of it at home.

Why this fits everything the Gulf has learned

Scholars call the Gulf’s post-2011 posture flexible cooperation: after the muted Arab Spring response, the 2014–16 oil shock, exclusion from talks, and the Afghanistan withdrawal, Gulf states swapped broad umbrellas for specific commitments tied to specific economics — Bahrain’s , F-35s linked to the , a U.S.–Saudi treaty perpetually under negotiation. It goes further: this is the weaponization of manufactured dependence — a capital-rich state cultivating a superpower’s reliance in order to steer it. It is also an economic bet: both Visions 2030 promise a post-oil economy, and always-on inference is the closest thing to a new barrel — though regional observers caution AI alone won’t carry the transition.

CHAPTER 5≈2.5 MIN

The Gulf’s Dilemma

The same infrastructure meant to summon American protection is what now draws the fire.

The security dilemmaMarch 2026

The promise

  • Compute as magnet — critical assets pull in troops and air defense
⇄

The price

  • Compute as target — dual-use campuses invite the strikes they should deter

In late February and early March 2026, after the U.S.–Israeli air campaign against Iran, Tehran answered with hundreds of missile and drone strikes across the GCC. Among them: two Amazon data centers in the UAE, one in Bahrain — AWS infrastructure behind Claude Gov and other models used for U.S. national security. They stand as history’s first strikes justified explicitly by AI’s military utility. The precedent came a year earlier: an Iranian ballistic missile on a U.S. base in Qatar.

Why data centers make sense as targets

“Cloud” hides how physical — and fragile — this infrastructure is. Cooling consumes roughly 40% of a data center’s electricity; interrupt power and GPUs overheat within seconds. An oft-cited 2007 industry study — predating modern redundancy — found 90% of data-center businesses failed within two years of a major outage. An attacker needn’t breach the building: cut electricity — sabotage, cyber, strike — and a multibillion-dollar facility goes dark.

And these now look like legitimate targets. Commercial clouds openly support military AI, intelligence storage, and operational planning; Iran justified the AWS strikes on precisely these grounds. The precedents existed: Russia’s 2022 strike on Ukraine’s government data centers — restored from backups, viable for stored weights, impossible for live inference and — and a 2024 Ukrainian hack that reportedly “destroyed” a center serving Russia’s military. AWS is the primary cloud carrying Anthropic’s Claude Gov models to the military via ; per former White House AI adviser Benjamin Buchanan — they remained in active use against Iran as of April 2026.

A quieter vulnerability: the grid gear itself. China, India, and Japan dominate electrical steel and substation components; DOE’s 2024 supply-chain review warned of “foreign entities of concern” controlling critical upstream materials. Add the hosts’ China ties — alleged G42 software transfers to Huawei, Blue Sword naval drills, Chinese drones and missiles in Gulf arsenals — and the warning lands: the Gulf solved America’s bottlenecks by substituting its own — instability, proven Iranian reach, Chinese leverage.

CHAPTER 6≈3 MIN

Where Chips Are Going

Biden treated chips as leverage to hoard. Trump treats them as market share to seize. The Gulf deals are the hinge.

Two theories of the chip2025

Biden · control

  • Three tiers, hard caps, compute as leverage
  • Slow China even at cost to U.S. sales
⇄

Trump · diffusion

  • Bilateral deals, crowd rivals out of the market
  • Sell the frontier to partners; keep embargoing China’s access to it

Biden’s (January 15, 2025) sorted the world into three tiers: 18 close allies, unrestricted; a broad middle — Saudi Arabia and the UAE included — capped near 50,000 each through 2027, contingent on security agreements; adversaries, chiefly China, at zero. No U.S. company could place more than 7% of its compute in any Tier-2 country. On the rule’s compliance deadline — May 15, 2025 — Commerce instead unveiled the UAE/U.S. framework, publicly scrapping it.

What about China? The stakes, quantified

DeepSeek proved algorithmic efficiency can partially substitute for compute — with a lag. But the frontier runs on — which chips cross which borders is the whole game. The Institute for Progress calculated that selling China even the — a half-performance, half-price Blackwell — would shrink America’s compute lead from 31× to under 4×; prior-generation H200 access alone would multiply China’s total compute ~2.5×. The Gulf deals keep Blackwell embargoed for Beijing — but every chip abroad tests the seals.

Why sell at all? Jensen Huang calls China a “$50-billion opportunity”; it was 25% of Nvidia’s data-center revenue before the 2023 controls. Trump’s wager: flood partners with American silicon, lock in the U.S. ecosystem, crowd out Huawei — while smuggling through Malaysia and Singapore and rent-a-GPU workarounds keep testing whether “embargoed” still means anything. The Gulf deals are the doctrine’s first full-scale experiment.

The bottom line

Historic in kind, still small in delivered fact. One UAE campus alone equals about a third of U.S. operating data-center capacity. Pledged capital exceeds $2 trillion.

But measure delivery, not announcements: six months of review produced ≈49 MW of approvals per country against a 5,000 MW promise — a pace that prices out at 50+ years. In chips, the realignment is about 1% built.

The verdict is unambiguous: shortsighted — the risks outweigh the benefits. Its own caveat: “too soon to tell” whether pledges become infrastructure, so the ledger below weighs commitments, not outcomes.

The risks (as argued)

  • Kinetic exposureDense campuses in missile range — March 2026 proved intent, capability, precedent.
  • Chinese leverageHosts trade 4× more with Beijing than Washington; espionage and transfer risks follow the chips.
  • Entanglement by designManufactured dependence pulls U.S. forces into defending someone else’s deterrent.
  • Swapped bottlenecksPermitting delays traded for war risk and adversary-adjacent supply chains.

The opportunities (as argued)

  • Speed America lacksGigawatts, land, permits at sovereign pace while U.S. queues run five years.
  • Cheap, subsidized power3.2–7.4¢/kWh, with future prices expected even lower.
  • Capital at scale$2T+ pledged into the U.S. economy and AI stack.
  • Market lock-in vs. HuaweiAmerican silicon and clouds become the Gulf default before China’s can.

China is the reason and the risk at once. The deals exist to out-diffuse Huawei and keep the Gulf on the American stack; Blackwell stays embargoed for Beijing. Yet the hosts are deeply China-entangled — $100B+ Saudi–China trade, joint naval drills, Chinese missiles in Gulf arsenals, a documented G42–Huawei transfer allegation.

The margin is thin: cited modelling puts the U.S. lead at 31× — collapsing to <4× if even half-strength Blackwells reached China. Every chip abroad tests enforcement already failing at the edges.

“The U.S. is trading the integrity of its potentially most consequential technology for access to electricity, regulatory permission, and capital it could, with sufficient political will, generate at home.”— THE BOTTOM LINE

The arms-for-oil story, come full circle: in 1945, America traded protection for a resource it feared running out of. In 2025, it traded its scarcest technology for resources — electrons, acreage, patience — it never truly lacked. Whether the bargain looks wise in 2035 depends on wars not yet fought, pledges not yet paid, and chips still in Commerce’s queue.
